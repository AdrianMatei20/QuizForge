The outer shell of the Earth, the lithosphere, is broken into large pieces called tectonic plates. The plates float on a hotter, softer layer of rock beneath them called the asthenosphere, and they move a few centimetres each year, about as fast as fingernails grow.

Plates meet at three kinds of boundaries. At a divergent boundary, two plates move apart. Molten rock rises from below to fill the gap and cools into new crust. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a divergent boundary, so the Atlantic Ocean slowly gets wider.

At a convergent boundary, two plates move towards each other. When an ocean plate meets a continental plate, the denser ocean plate sinks beneath the other one in a process called subduction. Subduction forms deep ocean trenches and chains of volcanoes. When two continental plates collide, neither sinks easily, so the crust crumples and is pushed up into high mountain ranges such as the Himalayas.

At a transform boundary, two plates slide past each other sideways. Crust is neither created nor destroyed there, but the plates often stick and then slip suddenly, which causes earthquakes. The San Andreas Fault in California is a transform boundary.

Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen near plate boundaries, because that is where plates interact. Places far from any boundary usually have few earthquakes.

The theory of plate tectonics grew from the older idea of continental drift. Evidence for it includes the matching shapes of coastlines such as those of South America and Africa, and the same fossils found on continents that are now separated by oceans.
