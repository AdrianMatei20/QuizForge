In a market, the price of a good depends on supply and demand. Demand is how much of a good buyers want to buy at each price. Supply is how much sellers want to sell at each price.

The law of demand says that, when other things stay the same, buyers want less of a good as its price rises. The law of supply says that sellers want to sell more of a good as its price rises, because selling it becomes more profitable.

The equilibrium price is the price at which the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer. If the price is above equilibrium, sellers offer more than buyers want, which creates a surplus, and sellers lower their prices to sell their stock. If the price is below equilibrium, buyers want more than is offered, which creates a shortage, and prices rise.

A change in price moves buyers and sellers along their existing demand and supply curves. Other changes shift the whole curve. Demand shifts when buyers' incomes, tastes or the prices of related goods change. For example, if the price of coffee rises, demand for tea, a substitute, increases. Supply shifts when production costs, technology or the number of sellers change.

When demand increases and supply stays the same, both the equilibrium price and the quantity sold rise. When supply increases and demand stays the same, the equilibrium price falls and the quantity sold rises.

Price elasticity of demand measures how strongly the quantity demanded reacts to a change in price. Demand for goods with few substitutes, such as basic medicines, is inelastic: buyers keep buying even when the price rises. Demand for goods with many substitutes is elastic.
