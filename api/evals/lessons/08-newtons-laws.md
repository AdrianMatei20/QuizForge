Isaac Newton described three laws that explain how forces change the motion of objects.

The first law, the law of inertia, says that an object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at a constant speed, unless a net force acts on it. Inertia is an object's resistance to a change in its motion. A passenger in a car that brakes suddenly keeps moving forward, because nothing has yet acted on the passenger to slow them down.

The second law says that the acceleration of an object is equal to the net force on it divided by its mass. It is often written as F = m × a. Pushing an object with twice the force gives it twice the acceleration, while an object with twice the mass gets only half the acceleration from the same force. Force is measured in newtons: one newton accelerates a mass of one kilogram by one metre per second every second.

The net force is the sum of all the forces on an object, taking their directions into account. If two people push a box with equal force in opposite directions, the net force is zero and the box does not accelerate.

The third law says that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. When one object pushes on another, the second object pushes back on the first with a force of the same size in the opposite direction. A swimmer pushes the water backwards, and the water pushes the swimmer forwards. The two forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other out.

Friction is a force that opposes motion between surfaces in contact. It is the reason a ball rolling on grass eventually stops, which can make it look as if moving objects naturally slow down, even though, by the first law, they would keep moving without it.
