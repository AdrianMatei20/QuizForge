The immune system protects the body against pathogens, which are microbes such as bacteria and viruses that cause disease.

The first line of defence is a set of barriers. The skin blocks most pathogens from entering the body. Mucus in the nose and throat traps microbes, and acid in the stomach kills many of those that are swallowed.

Pathogens that get past the barriers meet the innate immune system. It responds within minutes or hours, and it responds the same way to every invader. White blood cells called phagocytes engulf and digest microbes. Damaged tissue releases chemicals that cause inflammation: the area becomes red, warm and swollen as more blood and white blood cells arrive.

The adaptive immune system is slower, taking several days the first time it meets a pathogen, but it is specific to that pathogen. It relies on white blood cells called lymphocytes. B cells make antibodies, which are proteins that attach to a particular antigen, a molecule on the surface of the pathogen, and mark the pathogen for destruction. T cells find and destroy the body's own cells that have been infected.

After an infection, some B and T cells remain as memory cells. If the same pathogen enters the body again, the memory cells recognise it and the adaptive response is much faster and stronger, often fast enough that the person never feels ill. This is called immunity.

Vaccines use this memory. A vaccine contains a harmless form or part of a pathogen, such as a weakened virus or one of its proteins. The immune system responds to it and makes memory cells, without the person having to catch the disease first.

Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them from growing, but they do not work against viruses.
